---
title: Settlement and reorganization risk
---


This page explains the difference between a transaction Arch Network has authorized and a
transaction Bitcoin has settled, and what lives in the gap between them. Everything on this
page is Arch Network and Bitcoin; nothing on it is bounded by anything Arch Prime does. The
technical paper is authoritative on the architecture.

## Pre-confirmation is not Bitcoin finality

Agreement on Arch Network application state does not by itself move BTC. Authorization of the
Bitcoin transaction is a separate step. And an authorized transaction is not a settled one.

Pre-confirmation is not Bitcoin finality. A transaction can leave the mempool before
confirmation, and a Bitcoin reorganization can alter recently confirmed state.

Those are two distinct ways for a pre-confirmed transaction to fail to become a settled one,
and they have different causes, different likelihoods and different remedies.

## Two latencies, never one number

There are two latencies in this system and they must not be conflated:

- **Signing and coordination latency** at the Arch Network layer — the time from an action
  being agreed to a valid signature existing.
- **Bitcoin settlement latency** — the time from broadcast to sufficient confirmation depth
  on Bitcoin.

They have different distributions, different variances and different failure modes. The
first is a property of the validator set and its coordination. The second is a property of
Bitcoin's block production and its fee market, and it is outside anybody's control.

Any single unqualified latency figure for Arch Network is a conflation of the two, and this
documentation does not state one. When a number is given for either, it will be named as
which.

> **[OPEN — no latency figure is stated in this set. The measured signing and coordination figure has not been sourced, and Bitcoin settlement latency is a distribution rather than a value. Neither may be published as a single number, and no combined figure exists · owner: Protocol Engineering]**

## The residual exposure window

Between pre-confirmation and sufficient confirmation depth, the account's on-chain state is
provisional. During that window:

- a liquidation that has been authorized may not have settled;
- collateral that appears released may not be released;
- a deposit that appears credited may be undone;
- a repayment that closes a position may not yet be final.

Anyone who acts on a pre-confirmed state during that window is extending credit for its
duration, whether or not they describe it that way. Specifically:

- **A liquidator** who has delivered value against a pre-confirmed collateral transfer is
  exposed until that transfer settles.
- **A lender** whose repayment is pre-confirmed still holds the credit exposure until it
  confirms.
- **A client** who has been shown a released balance holds a balance that has been promised,
  not delivered.

The window's length is a policy choice — how deep the system requires confirmation before
treating an action as irreversible. Requiring more depth reduces reorganization exposure and
increases the time during which collateral is provisional and capital is tied up. It is a
trade with no correct answer, only a chosen one.

> **[OPEN — confirmation depth policy is not set: how many confirmations are required before each class of action (collateral release, liquidation settlement, deposit crediting, position closure) is treated as final, and whether depth requirements vary by size · owner: Protocol Engineering / Risk]**

## Mempool eviction

A signed transaction sitting in the mempool is not a settled transaction, and it is not
guaranteed a place in a block. It can be evicted — most commonly because a fee that was
adequate when the transaction was constructed is inadequate by the time blocks are being
filled.

Fee markets rise for the same reasons that produce liquidations: many participants
transacting at once, in a hurry. Eviction risk is therefore highest during exactly the events
that make settlement urgent, which is the pattern this entire section keeps repeating.

An evicted transaction has to be replaced, which means it returns to the signing layer to be
re-signed and re-broadcast, which means it re-acquires the participation requirement
described in [4.7](./4.7-protocol-validator-and-upgrade-risk). A liquidation that is
evicted during a fast drawdown must be re-signed and re-broadcast into a fee market that is
still rising, against a price that is still moving.

Settlement appears on Bitcoin as an ordinary Taproot-signed transaction with no
distinguishing marker. That is a compatibility and privacy property, and it means the
transaction receives no special treatment from miners, no priority, and no protection from
the fee market. Arch Network's transactions queue like everyone else's.

## Reorganization detection and rollback

Programs reference live Bitcoin state, because the ArchVM indexes Bitcoin's chain and
mempool. When Bitcoin reorganizes, the facts those programs referenced change underneath
them: a transaction that was confirmed is no longer in the canonical chain, an output that
existed does not, and an ordering the application relied on has been rewritten.

Detection is a function of how deep the system watches and how quickly it reconciles.
Rollback means application state computed against the orphaned history has to be recomputed
against the new one. That recomputation is not a rewind — the world has continued to move
while it happens, and the outcome depends on a reconciliation policy that determines which
side of a discrepancy is honoured.

## What a rollback means for a position

**A position liquidated in the affected window.** The on-chain closeout may be reversed while
its market effect is not. The position can be restored to the account and the collateral
restored to its lock, but the sale happened: someone bought, at a price, and that trade is a
completed market event. If the liquidation is then re-run against the new canonical history,
it re-runs into a market that has already absorbed the first one. The client is exposed to
the difference, and whoever took the other side of the reversed closeout holds either a claim
or a loss depending on how reconciliation resolves.

**A position opened in the affected window.** Borrow-and-deploy happens as one atomic bundle
— validate, issue debt, deposit into the strategy, mint the receipt into the margin account,
register the position, recompute health and assert it, or the whole bundle reverts.

That atomicity is a property of the bundle at the Arch Network layer. It is not a property of
the Bitcoin history the bundle was computed against. The revert guarantee covers the case
where a step inside the bundle fails. It does not cover the case where a step succeeded and the
Bitcoin state it depended on was subsequently reorganized away.

The failure that follows is the one a risk officer should focus on: an account holding a debt
and a strategy receipt, against a collateral lock that is no longer in the canonical chain —
or the mirror image, a collateral lock with no corresponding debt. Which of those occurs, and
how it is resolved, is determined by reconciliation policy rather than by the atomicity of
the bundle.

> **[OPEN — reorganization reconciliation policy is not defined: reorg depth monitored, how application state is recomputed against a new canonical history, how a reversed liquidation is settled with the counterparty who took the other side, and how a bundle whose collateral lock is reorganized away is resolved · owner: Protocol Engineering / Risk]**

## Who bears it

Whoever acted on pre-confirmation. Which party that is depends entirely on the action: the
liquidator on a collateral transfer, the lender on a repayment, the client on a release. In
a reorganization, the party bearing the loss is decided by a reconciliation policy that is
not yet written.

## What reduces it

- Confirmation-depth requirements before an action is treated as irreversible.
- The separation of application agreement from Bitcoin authorization, which means Arch
  Network's application state does not commit Bitcoin until the authorization step runs.
- Reorganization detection at the indexing layer, since the ArchVM is already following
  Bitcoin's chain and mempool.
- Fee management and replacement logic on broadcast transactions.

## What does not reduce it

- **Speed at the Arch Network layer.** A faster signing layer shortens the interval before
  broadcast. It does nothing to the interval before Bitcoin finality, and the second
  interval is the one that carries the risk. Improvements to the first can make the exposure
  window feel shorter while leaving it unchanged.
- **The atomic bundle.** It guarantees all-or-nothing within a bundle. It does not guarantee
  the durability of the Bitcoin state that bundle was computed against.
- **Standard Taproot settlement.** No special treatment, no priority, no exemption from the
  fee market.
- **Rule-based enforcement.** It determines that a closeout begins. It does not determine
  when Bitcoin will confirm it.

## The residual

Every transaction in this system passes through a window in which it has been authorized and
not settled, and during that window it can be evicted, delayed, or reorganized away. Deep
reorganizations are rare on Bitcoin. They are not impossible, the depth at which the system
stops treating them as possible has not been set, and the policy that would determine who
absorbs the loss when one occurs has not been written.
