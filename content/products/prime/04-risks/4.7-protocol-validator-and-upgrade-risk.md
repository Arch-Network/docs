title: Protocol, validator and upgrade risk
---


This page states the assumptions Arch Network rests on and what happens when each of them
fails. The technical paper is authoritative on the architecture; this page summarizes only
what a risk assessment turns on.

Almost everything here is Arch Network risk. It is not Arch Prime risk, and the difference
is not cosmetic: no decision Arch Prime makes bounds a validator coalition, and no property
of the network bounds a parameter Arch Prime sets badly. A client is exposed to both. The
section headed "Two authorities, not one" below says where the line falls.

## The principal risk

**If participants controlling more than the signing threshold collude, they can authorize
transactions the rules do not permit.**

This is not one risk among several. It is the assumption the system rests on, and every
other property described anywhere in this documentation — enforcement that does not depend
on Arch Prime, seizure rights capped and automatic, collateral that stays on Bitcoin — holds
conditional on it. A reader who takes away one sentence from this section should take away
this one.

## How authorization works

Arch Network's validators run delegated proof of stake. Signing authority is distributed
using FROST threshold Schnorr signatures, with ROAST for robustness when signers are
unavailable or adversarial. Both are published constructions from the peer-reviewed
literature; Arch Network did not invent them. Its contribution is their operation as the live
authorization layer of a system holding native Bitcoin collateral.

Key shares are distributed in proportion to stake weight. No single operator — including
Arch Network's own developers — can move collateral unilaterally.

Two properties of that design matter for risk.

**Agreement is not authorization.** Agreement on Arch Network application state does not by
itself move BTC. Authorization of the Bitcoin transaction is a separate step. Conflating them is
the most common misreading of the architecture, and it matters here because the two steps
fail differently: consensus can be healthy while signing is not, and a transaction that the
application state says should happen is not a transaction that has been authorized.

**An unauthorized signature is indistinguishable on Bitcoin from an authorized one.** FROST
produces standard Schnorr signatures, so settlement appears on Bitcoin as an ordinary
Taproot-signed transaction with no distinguishing marker. That is a desirable privacy and
compatibility property, and it has a direct consequence for detection: Bitcoin will accept a
spend produced by a colluding threshold exactly as it accepts a legitimate one, and no
observer of the Bitcoin chain can tell the difference by looking at the transaction. If
collusion is detected at all, it is detected at the Arch Network layer, by comparing what was
signed against what the rules permitted. Bitcoin will not help.

## Safety failure: collusion above threshold

**What it costs.** Collateral moved contrary to the rules. That is the full loss case for
every client whose Bitcoin is locked under the relevant spending conditions, and the full
loss case for lenders whose recovery depends on those conditions being enforceable.

**What bounds it.** The distribution of stake weight, and the cost of acquiring or
corrupting a threshold share of it. That is the specific assumption, stated instead of
claiming there is no trust in the system: you are trusting that a threshold-sized coalition
of stake-weighted signers does not form, and that the cost of forming one exceeds what a
coalition would gain from the collateral it could reach.

That is an economic assumption, not a cryptographic one. The cryptography is what makes a
sub-threshold coalition unable to act. Nothing in the cryptography prevents a
super-threshold coalition from acting.

**What does not bound it.** The client's self-custody. This is the point where the custody
property must be read precisely. The client retains custody and enforcement does not require
possession — the collateral is an unspent output on Bitcoin, locked under predefined Taproot
spending conditions, never reissued elsewhere. But those spending conditions delegate spend
authority to the threshold key. A colluding threshold does not need to take possession of
anything. It satisfies the condition, and the condition is what the client agreed to when
they locked the output.

## Liveness failure: participation below threshold

Below the participation threshold, authorization halts.

That prevents unauthorized movement. It also prevents authorized movement.

**What halts.** Liquidations that should be executing. Withdrawals a client has requested.
Repayments a borrower is attempting. Bundle openings, closures, and any other action whose
final step is an authorized Bitcoin transaction. The application layer can continue to agree
about what should happen and be unable to make it happen.

**Why it is worse than an outage.** A halted exchange stops trading. A halted authorization
layer stops the remedy while the market keeps moving. An account that crossed its trigger
during the halt is not liquidated on time; it is liquidated when signing resumes, at
whatever price then obtains. Time-bounded enforcement is time-bounded only while the signing
layer is live, and a halt converts a bounded closeout into an unbounded one. The bound was
always conditional on liveness; this is the condition failing.

The correlation is the difficulty. The events most likely to stress validator participation
— network incidents, coordinated attacks, extreme volatility driving operational load — are
events during which liquidation is most needed.

**What reduces it.** ROAST is specifically for robustness when signers are unavailable or
adversarial, and it reduces the frequency with which a signing session fails to complete in
the presence of faulty participants. It does not remove the participation requirement. Below
threshold, there is no protocol that produces a signature.

## Upgrade authority

**Upgrade authority over the programs is a real central point of failure.**

Stated without softening: whoever holds upgrade authority over Arch Network's programs can
change the programs that define what collateral is, how health is computed, what the
haircuts are, what the liquidation ranks are, which strategies are whitelisted, and under
what conditions collateral may be moved. Those programs are the rules. Authority over the
rules sits above the rules and is not constrained by them.

This means every enforcement statement in this documentation carries an implicit qualifier,
and the honest way to read it is:

> ...given the programs as currently deployed.

A client who has evaluated the account model, the liquidation logic and the parameters has
evaluated a specific deployment. The property that enforcement is non-discretionary and
survives Arch Prime's insolvency is a statement about what happens if Arch Prime stops
existing. It is not a statement about what whoever holds upgrade authority over the network's
programs can change while the network is running.

## Two authorities, not one

A client is exposed to two different powers over the same account, held by two different
parties, and merging them understates both.

**Arch Network's upgrade authority** changes the rules. It can redefine what collateral is,
how health is computed, and the conditions under which collateral moves. It sits above the
account rules, it is not constrained by them, and who holds it is unspecified — which is what
the flag above records.

**Arch Prime's parameter and underwriting discretion** operates within those rules. It sets
the haircut on a position, the per-account borrow cap, the liquidation rank, and the max LTV;
it decides which strategy is whitelisted and which counterparty is onboarded. These are
business judgements exercised inside the programs rather than changes to them.

The distinction matters because the failure modes and the remedies differ. A bad parameter is
visible, published, and checkable against your own account before you open a position, and
its worst case is bounded by what the programs allow. A change to the programs is none of
those things: it can alter what the programs allow, including retroactively for positions
already open, and no amount of diligence on Arch Prime's parameters anticipates it.

They also fail independently. Arch Prime can set every parameter correctly and still be a
client of a network whose upgrade authority is exercised against them. See
[4.5](./4.5-counterparty-risk), which lists both as counterparties.

## Implementation risk

The constructions are published and analysed. The implementation is Arch Network's own.

That distinction is the risk. A correct construction implemented incorrectly fails as
completely as an incorrect construction, and the published analysis of FROST and ROAST says
nothing about the code that instantiates them here.

The trusted path is wider than the signature scheme:

- **The programs themselves**, which encode collateral rules, health computation and the
  liquidation walk.
- **The ArchVM**, an eBPF-based environment written against in Rust, in which those programs
  run.
- **The Bitcoin indexer.** The ArchVM indexes Bitcoin's chain and mempool so programs can
  reference live Bitcoin state. A program that is served incorrect chain or mempool state
  computes correctly on wrong facts, and the resulting transaction is fully authorized. The
  indexer is part of the trusted computing base, not infrastructure alongside it.
- **The signing coordination layer**, where a session's participants, message construction
  and nonce handling live.

Security work is a gate on capacity rather than completed work. Opening a capacity rung
requires a second audit, formal verification of the liquidation path, and a funded bug
bounty. A reader should take that as the current status: those are conditions to be met, and
until they are met the implementation has not been through them.

## Who bears it

Everyone, in different directions.

- A **safety failure** takes client collateral and lender principal together, and there is
  no recovery mechanism described anywhere in this set that operates after collateral has
  left under a valid signature.
- A **liveness failure** falls on whichever party needs a transaction during the halt.
  During a drawdown that is the lender, whose liquidation cannot execute. During a
  withdrawal request that is the client, whose collateral cannot be released.
- An **implementation failure** falls wherever the defect points, and by definition is not
  known in advance.
- An **upgrade** falls on every open position at once.

None of these is borne by Arch Prime, and Arch Prime cannot make any of them good. The client
and the lenders absorb them directly.

## What reduces it

- Stake-weighted distribution of key shares, so that no single operator can act alone,
  including any operator Arch Network runs itself.
- Published, peer-reviewed constructions rather than proprietary cryptography.
- ROAST, which keeps signing sessions completing in the presence of unavailable or
  adversarial participants.
- The separation of application agreement from Bitcoin authorization, which means an
  incorrect application state does not by itself move collateral.
- The audit, formal verification and bug bounty gate, once it is closed.

## What does not reduce it

- **Bitcoin.** The base layer validates a signature. It does not validate the legitimacy of
  the process that produced it, and a threshold-signed spend is an ordinary Taproot spend to
  every node on the network.
- **Holding collateral as a native UTXO.** It is the correct design and it does not help
  here, because the spending condition is the thing being satisfied.
- **Haircuts, ranks and caps.** They are parameters inside the programs. They do not
  constrain a party who can change the programs, and they do not constrain a coalition
  authorizing a transaction the programs never approved.
- **Arch Network's own key custody.** No single operator can move collateral unilaterally,
  which addresses unilateral action and not collusion.
- **Arch Prime's underwriting and parameters.** They operate inside the programs. They do
  not constrain a validator coalition, a halt, or a change to the programs themselves.
- **Insurance, backstop or recovery funds.** None is described in this set.

## The residual

A client of Arch Prime is making a trust assumption about a threshold-sized coalition of
Arch Network's stake-weighted signers, and a second trust assumption about an unspecified
upgrade authority over the network's programs, in an implementation that has not completed
its second audit or the formal verification of its liquidation path. Those assumptions are
about the network, and the client is making them on top of, not instead of, the underwriting
and parameter discretion they are taking from Arch Prime.

Two of those three are flagged above as unresolved. Until they are closed, the assumption a
client is being asked to make has not been fully written down.
