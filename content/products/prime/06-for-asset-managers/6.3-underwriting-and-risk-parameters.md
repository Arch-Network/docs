This page answers how a strategy is assessed, what Arch Prime will ask you for, and what
the resulting parameters mean arithmetically for the clients deployed into you.

A lending protocol prices collateral. A prime broker prices a counterparty. That is a
structural distinction, not a quality claim, and Arch Prime sits on the second side of it
while running on Arch Network, an independent Layer 1 whose programs execute in the ArchVM
and index Bitcoin's chain directly. Underwriting a strategy for Prime is therefore
counterparty work: the
object being assessed is you, your process, your custody chain and your ability to return
money on a schedule — not only the asset the account ends up holding.

## The haircut function

```
haircut = f(price volatility, enforcement certainty, unwind route)
```

Three inputs, and they are not weighted equally.

**Price volatility.** How much the position's value moves, and how well the valuation
source tracks it. A NAV published at a slow cadence carries the volatility of the interval
between publications, not the volatility of the reported series.

**Enforcement certainty.** Whether the seizure and closeout path is capped, automatic and
dependent on nobody's cooperation. On the Bitcoin collateral leg it is, and it is Arch
Network that makes it so: the collateral remains an unspent transaction output under
predefined Taproot spending conditions, and authorization is distributed across the
network's validators in proportion to stake weight, so no single operator can move it
unilaterally. On your leg, enforcement certainty is a question about
your redemption mechanics and your custody chain, and it is answered by evidence, not by
architecture.

**Unwind route.** Decisive, and the input most often forgotten. It asks how the position
turns back into something that retires debt, inside the liquidation window. There are two
routes and they fail differently.

*Redemption is slow but contractual.* You are the counterparty. The timing is what you
committed to, the obligation sits with you, and if it fails there is at least a term to
point at. What it costs is time, and time is the thing a breached account does not have.

*Secondary depth is fast but conditional.* The receipt is sold rather than redeemed, and
the position closes at market speed without you being asked. What it depends on is a book
that owes nobody anything. Depth withdraws under stress, and the conditions that impair a
strategy are the conditions that thin the market for its receipt. **A strategy underwritten
on depth alone is underwritten on the route most likely to be gone when it is needed.**

So the underwriting asks about both, and does not accept one as a substitute for the other.
A deep market for the receipt is a real answer to the rank-1 question and it does not
discharge the redemption question, because a route that is available on every ordinary day
is not the same as a route that is available on the day of the cascade. Both routes are
worked through in [6.4](./6.4-redemption-terms-and-liquidation-rank).

The output is a discount applied to your position wherever it appears in a client's
collateral value. A larger haircut does not make a strategy with no working route out
usable. It makes a usable strategy less useful.

## What Arch Prime will ask you for

Expect these to be asked in writing, with evidence, and asked again when anything changes.

**The yield source, mapped to external cash flows.** Name what generates the return and
where the cash comes from. The mapping has to terminate outside Arch Prime. A return that
depends on the system's own activity is disqualifying under leverage, for the reason given
in [6.2](./6.2-eligibility-and-the-collateral-registry).

**Strategy capacity.** How much the strategy can absorb before the return degrades, how
you measure that, and what you do at the ceiling. Capacity matters more here than in a
normal allocation because Prime flow is levered: a dollar of client equity can arrive as
several dollars of subscription.

**The market for the receipt.** Where the receipt trades, who provides the liquidity, what
size has actually cleared, and what happened to all of that the last time the market was
disorderly. Say what you do not know as well as what you do; an unobserved market is not a
deep one. Underwriting treats depth as an observation with a shelf life, not as a term.

> **[OPEN — how secondary-market depth is measured and evidenced for underwriting is not
> defined: what observation window, what size, what venue coverage and what stress
> conditions a manager must present, and what depth would be sufficient · owner: Risk]**

**Historical drawdown.** The worst realized drawdown, its duration, and its cause. The
duration matters as much as the depth, because a levered account has to survive the
interval, not the average.

**The custody and counterparty model.** Who holds the assets at each step, under what
legal arrangement, and what happens to client property if any of those parties fails.

**Venue exposure.** Every venue the strategy touches, including any venue outside Arch
Network's enforcement perimeter. The network's enforcement property does not extend to
assets sitting at a third-party venue, and Arch Prime's underwriting has to see that
boundary explicitly rather than discover it.

**Behaviour in a stress event.** Not a scenario table. A description of what actually
happens: what gets sold, in what order, how long it takes, what you do about redemptions
arriving during it, and what your governing documents permit you to do that a client would
not expect — gates, side pockets, in-kind settlement, suspension.

**Model, execution and operational risk.** For a strategy described as market-neutral, the
hedge is against directional price movement and nothing else. Execution, model,
counterparty, venue and operational risk remain, and the yield can go to zero or negative.
The underwriting is interested in those, because they are the paths by which a position
that is not supposed to move, moves.

## The process itself

Underwriting today is done by hand, per strategy. V1 runs one whitelisted strategy, and it
was hand-underwritten. There is no published, repeatable programme behind it — no stated
criteria you can score yourself against, no committed timeline, no appeal path, and no
standing committee whose decisions are recorded in a form you can read.

That is a gap, and it is stated here rather than dressed as a process.

> **[OPEN — underwriting is run by hand per strategy and is not a published, repeatable programme; stated criteria, submission requirements, decision timelines and an appeal path do not exist · owner: Risk]**

> **[OPEN — there is no defined re-underwriting trigger or cadence: what change in a strategy forces reassessment, and what happens to live client positions while a reassessment is in progress · owner: Risk]**

## The arithmetic, from your side

This is the part worth internalizing before you decide whether Prime flow is flow you
want.

In a levered account the binding constraint is not Bitcoin's price. It is the deployed
position's NAV. For an account with equity `E` and deployed position `V`, the impairment
that wipes equity is:

```
E / V
```

That is your strategy's drawdown budget for that client. Not your risk limit — theirs.

*Illustrative arithmetic, not a product parameter:* an account posts 1 BTC at $100,000 as
collateral, deploys $500,000, and carries $500,000 of debt. Here `E / V` is
$100,000 / $500,000 = 20%. A 20% impairment in the strategy wipes the client at full
Bitcoin value. A 100% Bitcoin drawdown leaves the account whole.

Read the second sentence again. The client's Bitcoin can go to zero and the account
survives; your strategy can lose a fifth and the client is gone. The leverage the client
took is expressed as a claim on your drawdown, and the ratio is set by their leverage, not
by your volatility.

Leverage translates to loan-to-value like this:

| Leverage | Implied LTV |
|---|---|
| 1.5× | ≈ 33% |
| 2.0× | 50% |
| 3.0× | ≈ 67% |
| 4.0× | 75% |

An 80% max LTV puts a hard ceiling at 5.0×. As leverage rises, `E / V` falls — at 5.0×,
equity is a fifth of the deployed position, and the drawdown that removes it is
correspondingly small.

> **[OPEN — max LTV is stated as 80% per the 21 August gameplan and is contested across the corpus; the figure is not settled and no liquidation threshold figure is stated anywhere in this set · owner: Risk]**

## What you supply, what Arch Prime supplies, what does not exist yet

**You supply:** the evidence above, and a counterparty who will keep supplying it. Most of
what determines your haircut is documentation you either have or do not.

**Arch Prime supplies:** the assessment, the resulting registry parameters, and the
disclosure to clients of what those parameters are. Arch Prime also supplies the
constraint that the credit funding these positions comes from third-party liquidity
providers, which is why the underwriting is stricter than a balance-sheet lender's would
need to be — the lenders underwrite the enforcement, and the enforcement has to hold on
your leg too.

**Arch Network supplies:** the enforcement certainty on the collateral leg that those
lenders are underwriting.

**Not yet available:** the programme, the criteria, the timeline, and the parameter
values. All four are flagged above.

## What does not hold

- A completed underwriting is not a warranty. Arch Prime does not stand behind your NAV to
  clients, and a haircut is not a loss buffer Arch Prime funds.
- Enforcement certainty on the collateral leg does not transfer to your leg. Rule-based
  enforcement determines that closeout begins, not the price at which it completes.
- The V1 margin model is additive. There is no correlation modelling and no netting
  against a client's other exposures, so no diversification argument reduces your haircut.
- None of the parameters here are published values. Treat every number on this page as
  arithmetic or as flagged.
