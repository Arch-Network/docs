---
title: Liquidity and unwind risk
---


This page explains why the route out of a position, rather than its quoted price, decides
what it is worth to a levered account — what the two routes are, how each one fails, and
what happens on the lending side when many suppliers want out at once.

## There are two routes out, and they fail differently

A deployed position is not exited one way. There are two, and the account's exposure is to
both of them at once because it cannot be certain in advance which one it will be using.

**Sale.** The receipt minted into the margin account is a transferable instrument. In many
cases closeout, deleveraging and client exit are a swap of the receipt on a DEX rather than
a redemption and burn. This route is **fast but conditional**: it clears at the speed of a
transaction, and it clears only if there is a book to sell into. Depth is quoted by
participants who are under no obligation to keep quoting, and what it realizes is the
market's price rather than the strategy's net asset value.

**Redemption.** The position is redeemed with the strategy, the receipt is burned, and the
proceeds retire the debt. This route is **slow but contractual**: it runs on terms written
down in advance, and those terms are also where the gates live — notice periods, queues,
lock-ups, in-kind delivery options, and discretionary suspension rights.

The two failure modes are not variants of each other. One is *nobody is bidding*. The other
is *the door is closed for thirty days*. A position can be exposed to either, and a
position can be exposed to both at the same time, because the conditions that empty a book
and the conditions that trigger a gate are the same conditions.

## The unwind route is the decisive haircut input

A haircut is set as a function of three things:

```
haircut = f(price volatility, enforcement certainty, unwind route)
```

The unwind route is the decisive one, and it is the input most often forgotten. It is
forgotten because the other two are visible on a screen. Volatility is measurable from
history. Enforcement certainty is a property of the account's own mechanics. The unwind
route is partly a term in a document and partly a book that may or may not be there on the
day, and neither appears on any chart.

It is decisive because a position that cannot be turned into repayment inside the window,
by one route or the other, is not collateral in the sense the health factor assumes. It is
an asset the account owns and cannot use.

And the two forms of the input do not fail together, which is the part that gets
underwritten wrong. Redemption is slow but contractual: you know what it costs in time, and
the thing that can defeat it is written down before you need it. Secondary depth is fast
but conditional: it costs nothing in time, and the thing that defeats it is not written
down anywhere, because it is a decision other participants make in the moment.

**A position underwritten on depth alone is underwritten on the route most likely to be
gone when it is needed.** Depth is the input that looks best in calm conditions and is
least present in the state the haircut exists to survive. A haircut justified by an
observed book, with no workable redemption window behind it, is a haircut resting on the
weaker of the two routes.

## A position that cannot be unwound in the window cannot be rank 1

Liquidation walks positions by rank ascending until health is restored, and deployed
strategy positions are rank 1 precisely so the levered leg is reached before the client's
Bitcoin.

**A position cannot hold rank 1 unless it can be unwound inside the liquidation window, by
either route.** The test is unwindability, not redeemability. A position with a deep
secondary market can qualify despite long redemption terms; a position with neither a
workable redemption window nor executable depth cannot.

The consequence is the part that matters to a client. If the deployed leg cannot restore
health inside the window — because there was no bid and the redemption door was shut — the
walk continues to the next position in rank order, and in a V1 account the position after
the deployed leg is the Bitcoin. The unwind route therefore decides which asset absorbs the
loss. It is not an operational footnote in a strategy's documentation; it determines
whether the structure protects the collateral it was built to protect.

## Quoted price is not executable value

This applies to the sale route directly, and it is the whole of what that route delivers.

A quote is the price of the marginal unit, offered to a participant who may choose not to
trade. Executable value is what the whole position realizes when it is closed, by the
party who has to close it, at the moment they have to close it.

Three wedges separate them:

- **Size.** The position is not the marginal unit. Closing it walks the book.
- **Timing.** A forced closeout is not an opportunistic one. The account transacts when
  the health factor says so, not when the market is receptive.
- **Information.** A forced seller who must complete inside a known window is a forced
  seller other participants can price. The window that makes enforcement reliable is also
  public knowledge about your constraint.

There is an honest inversion here worth stating directly. The property that makes
enforcement work — that closeout is non-discretionary, begins on a rule, and depends on
nobody's cooperation — is the same property that guarantees the account is a price-taker
at the moment of closeout. Rule-based enforcement determines that closeout begins, not the
price at which it completes. You do not get the certainty without the price-taking. They
are one design decision seen from two sides.

## What the sale route realizes is market price, not NAV

The account is marked at NAV. A receipt sold into the market fetches what the book will
pay. Under a cascade, other liquidations are selling the same instrument into the same
book, and the gap between the mark and the price widens exactly when it matters.

That gap is a loss channel in its own right, separate from anything happening inside the
strategy, and it is set out in full in
[4.3 Strategy and NAV risk](./4.3-strategy-and-nav-risk). It belongs on this page as
the reason the sale route's conditionality is not merely a timing problem: when depth
thins, the route does not simply become slower, it becomes more expensive, and the account
pays the difference against a mark that has not moved.

## Size consumes depth, and slippage is not linear

Two accounts at identical LTV, holding identical assets, do not carry identical risk if
one is ten times the size of the other. The larger position consumes more depth on the way
out, and cost per unit rises as the order walks the book rather than staying flat.

Under stress the book is thinner to begin with and refills more slowly, so the same order
travels further for the same size. This is why a per-account borrow cap is expressed as a
share of available depth rather than as a fixed dollar amount: the constraint that matters
is not how much a client borrows, it is how much of the market's exit capacity that
client's liquidation would need.

Rationing is by per-account borrow cap expressed as a share of available depth, with the
recommendation to start at 2% and step down as depth grows. At a 2% cap, fifty accounts
can each draw their full cap.

## Parameters must be set against the liquidity that will remain, not the liquidity on the screen

Most visible depth is conditional. It is quoted by participants who withdraw it when
volatility rises, and volatility rises in the state the parameter exists to survive. A
haircut calibrated to observed spreads and observed depth in calm conditions has been
calibrated against a market state that will not exist at the moment the haircut is used.

The same applies to redemption, in the other direction. A manager's willingness to meet
redemptions promptly in normal conditions tells you nothing about the gate in the
documents. Discretionary suspension rights, notice periods, queues, lock-ups and in-kind
delivery options are the mechanisms that convert a liquid-looking position into an
unreachable one, and they activate on correlated triggers — which means they activate when
your account needs the position most.

So both routes are calibrated on evidence drawn from a state that does not obtain during
the event. Depth is measured in calm markets. Redemption behaviour is observed in calm
markets. The correlation is the point: the two routes are not independent, and an
underwriting that treats them as two chances at an exit is counting one chance twice.

The bound is specific: a haircut is a bound on how the position is valued, not a bound on
what it realizes. There is no parameter in this system that bounds realized price.

## The borrow side: utilisation and withdrawal

Arch Prime does not fund client financing. Third-party liquidity providers do. Their capital is
what the leverage is drawn from, and their ability to leave is a risk in both directions —
to them, and to every borrower whose position depends on continued supply.

Utilisation is the share of supplied capital currently borrowed. Target utilisation is
85%. The rate curve inflects at 90%, and 85% is targeted because it preserves withdrawal
headroom.

For a lender, that headroom is the whole story:

- **At 85% utilisation**, 15% of supplied capital is unborrowed and available to exit
  immediately. That is the buffer, and it is shared across every supplier who wants to
  leave at the same time.
- **Above the inflection**, borrowing rates rise steeply. That is designed to attract new
  supply and to push existing borrowers to repay. Both are responses on a delay. The rate
  curve is a price signal; it does not create withdrawable liquidity at the moment a lender
  asks for it.
- **At full utilisation**, no lender can withdraw at any price until a borrower repays or
  is liquidated. A high rate makes waiting expensive for the borrower. It does not make
  the capital present.

Capacity on the borrow side follows from the same arithmetic:

```
supportable user collateral = (committed depth × utilisation) ÷ (leverage − 1)
```

A user seeking leverage λ consumes `(λ − 1)` dollars of borrow per dollar of collateral.
Higher leverage is not merely riskier for the account taking it; it is more expensive in
shared capacity, which is why caps are expressed against depth. Committed lending dollars,
not code, gate the launch, and the launch is therefore a capacity ladder rather than a
date.

## Who bears it

- **Borrowers** bear execution shortfall in a forced closeout, bear the discount to NAV
  when the closeout runs by sale, and bear the risk that a deployed position cannot be
  unwound inside the window by either route.
- **Lenders** bear withdrawal delay at high utilisation, and bear credit loss if a
  closeout realizes less than the debt it was meant to repay.
- Both bear the correlation: the moment borrowers most need to get out is the moment
  lenders most want to withdraw, and it is also the moment the book is thinnest and the
  gates most likely to be down.

## What reduces it

- The unwind route as a direct input to the haircut, so illiquidity in either form is
  priced rather than assumed away.
- The rank rule, which refuses rank 1 to any position that cannot be reached in time by
  either route.
- The 85% utilisation target, which is set below the 90% inflection specifically to keep
  withdrawal headroom rather than to maximise deployed capital.
- Per-account borrow caps as a share of depth, which prevent any single liquidation from
  needing more of the market than exists.
- Capacity gating, which holds leverage capacity to committed and papered depth rather
  than to demand.

## What does not reduce it

- **Having two routes rather than one.** They fail on correlated triggers. Two exits from
  the same building do not help if the same fire closes both.
- **The rate curve.** It reprices liquidity. It does not manufacture it.
- **A high advertised yield on the supply side.** Rate is not evidence of depth, and at
  high utilisation a high rate is evidence of the opposite.
- **Self-custody of collateral.** Holding your own Bitcoin does not help you exit a
  deployed position, and does not help a lender withdraw.
- **Rule-based enforcement.** It fixes that the exit begins. It does not improve the price
  at which the exit clears, it does not create a buyer, and it does not open a redemption
  window a manager has gated.
- **Observed historical liquidity.** It is a measurement of a state that does not obtain
  during the event — and that is true of observed redemption behaviour as well as of
  observed depth.
- **The NAV mark.** It is what the account computes health from. It is not what the
  position realizes on the way out.

## The residual

A lender at high utilisation can be unable to withdraw for as long as the loans remain
outstanding, with no mechanism available to compel repayment other than price. A borrower
can be closed out at a level no haircut or parameter promised — either because the
redemption door was shut inside the window, or because the book that was going to absorb
the receipt was not there when the position had to go, or because it was there and paid a
price well below the NAV the account was marked at. If neither route completes inside the
window, the loss reaches the Bitcoin.

Three of the inputs a reader would need to size any of this — the length of the liquidation
window, the redemption terms of the V1 assets, and whether any executable secondary depth
exists for the receipt at all — are flagged above as unset. Which route a closeout will
actually take is unset too.
