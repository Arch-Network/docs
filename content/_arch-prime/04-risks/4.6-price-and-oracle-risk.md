---
title: Price and oracle risk
---


This page explains what happens when the rules are correct and the price they are evaluated
against is not.

## Every rule in the account is evaluated against an external input

The account computes:

```
collateralValue = Σ (quantity × price × (1 − haircut))
healthFactor    = collateralValue / totalDebt
```

Quantity is known to the system. Haircut is set by the system. Debt is issued by the
system. `price` is not. It arrives from outside, and every decision the account makes —
whether a borrow is permitted, whether a bundle asserts successfully, whether liquidation
triggers, which positions are walked — is a function of it.

A stale or manipulated price produces a correctly executed transaction against an incorrect
assessment.

That sentence is the whole page. The transaction is valid. The signature is valid. The
program ran the rule it was written to run and reached the answer the input implied. The
answer is wrong, and nothing in the system is in a position to notice, because there is no
state in which the account knows the price is wrong. The input *is* the account's knowledge
of the world.

**Enforcement fidelity does not extend to input fidelity.** Rule-based enforcement is a
statement about what the system does with what it is told. It is not a statement about what
it is told.

## The two error directions are not symmetric

**A price or NAV that is too high** overstates collateral value, overstates the health
factor, and delays or prevents liquidation. The account is levered beyond the parameters
that were underwritten, and nobody is alerted, because the number the alert would be based
on is the number that is wrong. When the input corrects, the correction arrives as a step
change into an account with no buffer. The loss lands on client equity and then on the
lenders who financed the position.

**A price or NAV that is too low** understates the health factor and triggers a liquidation
on an account that was not impaired. This one is worse than it looks, because it is
irreversible in a way the other direction is not. The closeout executes, the position is
sold, the debt is repaid, and the client's loss is realized. When the input corrects five
minutes later, the position does not come back. Bitcoin settlement is not reversible on
request. A false trigger is a permanent transfer of value from the client to whoever took
the other side.

Both directions are produced by the same defect. Only one of them is visible as a loss on
the day it happens.

## Staleness

Every input has an age. Between updates, the account values positions at a price that no
longer exists, and acts on that valuation with full authority.

The exposure is the product of two things: how long the interval is, and how fast the true
price is moving during it. Those are not independent. Update intervals are fixed in advance;
price velocity is highest during exactly the events that make liquidation necessary. The
worst staleness error available to the system occurs in the state the system most needs to
be accurate in. See [4.2](./4.2-leverage-and-market-risk) on fast drawdowns.

## Manipulation

An attacker who can move the reported price has two payoffs available. Push it down to
trigger liquidations and capture the flow. Push it up to borrow beyond what the parameters
would permit, and leave the shortfall behind.

The economics are simple and they are the design question: the cost of the attack is the
cost of moving the source, and the payoff is the size of the positions the input touches.
Thin sources, single sources, and sources that can be moved on a venue with low depth are
the exposure. The size of the book that depends on the input grows over time; the cost of
moving a thin source does not grow with it.

## Spot Bitcoin pricing is the easy case

Bitcoin is continuously traded on many independent venues with deep books, which makes
multi-source construction, median or trimmed-mean aggregation, deviation checks and
staleness bounds all straightforward to specify. It is a solved category of problem.

One distinction matters here, because the architecture invites the wrong inference.
Programs run in the ArchVM, which indexes Bitcoin's chain and mempool so programs can
reference live Bitcoin state. That gives the programs direct knowledge of *chain facts* —
which outputs exist, what is locked, what is in the mempool, how deep a transaction is
confirmed. It does not give them a price. Chain state and exchange rate are different
categories of information, and only the former arrives without a trusted reporter.

## NAV valuation is a different and harder problem

For deployed strategy positions the input is not a price at all. It is a NAV, and NAV is
computed rather than observed.

**Who computes it.** In the general case, the manager or an administrator engaged by the
manager — the party whose performance the number measures. That is not an accusation; it is
the standard arrangement in asset management, and the reason independent administration and
audit exist as separate functions. In a levered account it becomes a live risk rather than a
governance preference, because the number goes directly into a health computation that
decides whether the client is closed out.

**How often.** On the strategy's cycle, not the account's. A margin account evaluates
continuously. A NAV is struck periodically. Between strikes, a levered position is carried
at a figure produced by a process the account does not control and cannot re-derive.

**What a stale or self-reported NAV does to the health factor.** It flows straight into
collateral value. The haircut applied to it was set as a function of price volatility,
enforcement certainty, and the unwind route — not as a function of reporting
integrity, independence, or strike frequency. There is no term in the haircut for "the
manager marks their own book."

The failure sequence is: the strategy is impaired; the NAV has not yet moved; the health
factor stays comfortably above the trigger; nothing happens. When the NAV is struck, the
health factor steps down. Liquidation begins into whatever market produced the impairment,
and the `E / V` arithmetic in [4.3](./4.3-strategy-and-nav-risk) means that at the
leverage ceiling a one-fifth impairment has already consumed the entire equity before the
number that would have triggered the closeout was published.

**The dangerous NAV is the smooth one.** A NAV that moves erratically attracts a large
haircut and a nervous underwriter. A NAV that reports steady, plausible, mildly positive
returns attracts neither. Smoothness is a property of the reporting process at least as
often as it is a property of the underlying strategy, and the account cannot distinguish
between the two.

## Who bears it

An input that flatters the account costs the client their equity and then costs the lenders
their principal. An input that understates the account costs the client a position they
should still have held. In both cases the party bearing the loss is the party who did not
produce the number.

## What reduces it

- Multiple independent sources with deviation checks, so a single venue cannot set the
  account's view of the world.
- Explicit staleness bounds, so an input has a maximum age past which the system declines to
  act rather than acting on stale data.
- Haircuts, which absorb bounded valuation error.
- The atomic health assertion in the bundle, which evaluates the position against the input
  at the moment of opening and reverts if the rules are not satisfied.
- Independent administration and audit of a strategy's NAV, where a listing requires it.

## What does not reduce it

- **Haircuts.** A haircut widens the tolerance around a number. It does not detect that the
  number is wrong, and it is sized for volatility rather than for misreporting.
- **Multiple sources that share a root.** Aggregating five feeds that all derive from the
  same venue, or five reports that all originate with the same administrator, produces the
  appearance of independence and none of the substance.
- **Publication on chain.** Writing a self-reported NAV to a public ledger makes it
  auditable after the fact and does not make it true at the time. Publication is not
  verification.
- **Validator honesty.** The validator set attests that a message was signed under the
  protocol's rules. It attests nothing about whether the content of that message is
  accurate. A correctly signed wrong price is a wrong price. See
  [4.7](./4.7-protocol-validator-and-upgrade-risk).
- **Self-custody.** The client's Bitcoin is theirs throughout, including throughout a
  liquidation triggered by a bad input.

## The residual

With the oracle design and the NAV verification method both unresolved, a client cannot at
present evaluate the timeliness or the accuracy of the number their health factor is
computed from, and cannot determine who produced it or how often.

That is the state of the disclosure today. Until the two flags above are closed, the
correct assumption for a reader is that the input is the least specified part of the system
and the one with the shortest path to their collateral.
