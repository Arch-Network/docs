title: Settlement on Bitcoin
---


This page describes how a Bitcoin transaction that moves your collateral actually gets
authorized, and what "settled" does and does not mean at each stage. Everything on it is
Arch Network's job: authorization and settlement belong to the chain, not to the
brokerage built on it. The Arch technical whitepaper is authoritative on the
construction; this page summarizes it for a reader holding an Arch Prime account.

## Two things that are not the same thing

**Agreement on Arch Network application state does not by itself move BTC.**
Authorization of the Bitcoin transaction is a separate step. Conflating them is the most
common misreading of the architecture.

Arch Network is a Layer 1, an independent chain. Its programs run in the ArchVM, an
eBPF-based environment written against in Rust, which indexes Bitcoin's chain and
mempool so that programs can reference live Bitcoin state. When validators agree that
your account's health has breached the trigger Arch Prime's account rules define, what
exists at that moment is an agreed state: a decision, recorded on Arch Network. Your
coin has not moved. It moves only when a Bitcoin transaction spending that output is
separately authorized and broadcast, and only when Bitcoin confirms it.

Every question about timing, finality and reversibility below is a question about which of
these two steps you are asking about.

## Step one: validators agree

Arch Network's validators run delegated proof of stake. They agree on the application
state — the account, its positions, its debts, the health factor, and whether an
instruction is admissible.

This is where the rules are applied. The rules themselves — the haircuts, the trigger, the
ranks — are Arch Prime's account parameters; what happens here is the network agreeing on
whether an instruction satisfies them. An instruction that would leave an account below its
requirement is rejected at this step, before any Bitcoin transaction is contemplated.

## Step two: the signature is produced by a threshold, not a party

Signing authority is distributed using FROST threshold Schnorr signatures, with ROAST for
robustness when signers are unavailable or adversarial. Key shares are distributed in
proportion to stake weight.

Two consequences follow:

- **No single operator — including Arch's own developers — can move collateral
  unilaterally.** There is no key that authorizes a spend on its own. There is no
  administrative override that bypasses the threshold.
- **Availability is handled, not assumed.** ROAST exists so that signing completes even
  when some share-holders fail to participate or participate dishonestly, rather than
  stalling until every signer cooperates.

FROST and ROAST are published constructions from the peer-reviewed literature; Arch
Network did not invent them. Arch Network's contribution is their operation as the live
authorization layer of a system holding native Bitcoin collateral.

## Step three: it settles as an ordinary Bitcoin transaction

FROST produces standard Schnorr signatures, so settlement appears on Bitcoin as an ordinary
Taproot-signed transaction with no distinguishing marker. There is no special script type
to be filtered, no marker to be censored on, and nothing that identifies the spend as
collateral movement to an outside observer. Bitcoin validates it the way it validates any
other Taproot spend.

## Pre-confirmation is not finality

You will see account state update quickly. That is Arch Network state, and it is useful
— it is what lets the account respond, reject, and recompute in the time a user expects.
It is not Bitcoin settlement.

**A pre-confirmation is not Bitcoin finality.** A transaction can leave the mempool before
confirmation. And a Bitcoin reorganization can alter recently confirmed state: an output
you saw confirmed can be returned to an unconfirmed condition if the chain reorganizes
around it.

The practical rule is to treat Arch Network state as the operational truth for what your
account is permitted to do, and Bitcoin confirmation depth as the settlement truth for
whether value has moved. Anything depending on the second — withdrawal completion,
collateral release, closeout proceeds — is subject to Bitcoin's confirmation model, not
Arch Network's.

## Two latencies, never one number

There are two distinct latencies in this system and they must not be conflated:

- **Signing and coordination latency** — how long it takes validators to agree and produce
  a threshold signature.
- **Bitcoin settlement latency** — how long it takes the resulting transaction to confirm
  on Bitcoin, which is Bitcoin's property and not Arch Network's.

Any single unqualified speed figure is describing one of these and being read as the other.

## Limits

- **The honest-majority assumption is the principal risk.** If participants controlling
  more than the signing threshold collude, they can authorize transactions the rules do not
  permit. It is not one risk among several — it is the assumption the whole authorization
  layer rests on.
- **Upgrade authority over the programs is a real central point of failure**, and is
  disclosed as such rather than argued away.
- **Reorganization risk is not eliminated, only bounded by depth.** Arch Network does
not and cannot make Bitcoin's recent history immutable.
- **Settlement speed is not enforcement certainty.** Enforcement determines that closeout
  begins, not the price at which it completes — and the interval between the decision and
  the confirmed spend is market exposure that nobody removes.
