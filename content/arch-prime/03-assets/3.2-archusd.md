This page explains what archUSD is, what stands behind it, how it is created, what is and is not stated about getting out of it, and why its backing and its market liquidity are two separate things that answer two separate questions.

## The mechanism

archUSD is a 1:1 stablecoin-backed dollar with permissionless mint, custodied at BitGo.

One unit of archUSD exists for one dollar of stablecoin reserve held with the custodian. Mint is permissionless: anyone who delivers the reserve asset can create the token, without an approval step or a relationship with Arch Prime.

Mint is what the governing documents state. Redemption is not: no redemption mechanic — including whether one unit returns one dollar of the reserve asset — is stated anywhere, and it is flagged below rather than described here.

That is the whole instrument. archUSD wraps dollars that already arrived. It is plumbing.

Who issues it is a separate question, and this page cannot answer it. Issuing a token and holding a stablecoin reserve at a custodian is neither Arch Network's function — holding Bitcoin collateral and settling to Bitcoin — nor Arch Prime's — extending credit and underwriting counterparties. The rest of this page therefore names no responsible entity, and says "the issuer" where one would go.

## archUSD is not a CDP, and that has consequences

A collateralized debt position works the other way around. You post a volatile asset, the system mints a dollar-denominated liability against it, and a dollar that did not previously exist enters circulation, secured by collateral that can fall in value. The mint event *is* a credit event.

archUSD does none of that. It wraps dollars that already arrived. There is no borrower, no debt, no liquidation path attached to the token itself, and no collateral ratio to monitor. Minting archUSD does not create credit — it changes the form of a dollar that already existed somewhere else.

Three things follow, and each of them is a thing readers get wrong:

**Holding archUSD is not lending, and it is not a position.** You are not exposed to a borrower's health. You hold a claim on a reserve.

**The archUSD supply is not a measure of Arch Prime's lending capacity.** archUSD does not manufacture lending capacity. Capacity comes from committed lending dollars supplied by third-party liquidity providers, and it is those commitments, not the token supply, that gate what the book can finance.

**Growth in archUSD supply is not evidence of anything about the loan book.** It means more dollars were wrapped. It says nothing about utilisation, leverage, or credit quality.

## The reserve: what it is, and what it is not

**What it is.** A reserve of stablecoins held at BitGo, sized one-for-one against archUSD outstanding. That is what stands behind each unit; what a holder can do to reach it is the undescribed part.

**What it is not:**

- It is not a yield source. archUSD has no yield leg, and none is claimed. If you want dollar-denominated yield inside an Arch Prime account, that is a different instrument with a different risk profile — see 3.4.
- It is not diversified by design. It is stablecoin-denominated, so it inherits whatever the reserve assets themselves are exposed to.
- It is not a claim on Arch Prime's balance sheet. Arch Prime does not fund client financing; third-party liquidity providers do. The reserve is what stands behind archUSD, and there is no second line behind the reserve.
- It is not a bank deposit and carries no deposit insurance.

The reserve's composition and the cadence at which it is attested are not confirmed. Until they are, you cannot answer "what exactly am I holding a claim on, and how often is that verified by a party outside the issuer" — which is the first question a reader deciding on size should ask.

## Reserve risk

Reserve risk is the risk that the thing standing behind the token stops standing behind it at full value, or stops being reachable on the timetable you need.

It has three distinct paths, and they fail differently:

**Reserve asset risk.** The stablecoins in the reserve can break their own peg or become illiquid. archUSD's 1:1 accounting does not insulate the holder from this; it passes it through. A dollar of reserve that is worth ninety cents backs a dollar of archUSD with ninety cents.

**Custodian risk.** The reserve sits with BitGo. Custodian failure, freeze, or operational interruption interrupts redemption regardless of whether the assets are fully present. Arch Network's enforcement against Bitcoin collateral does not reach a custodied reserve; those are different mechanisms in different places.

**Process risk.** Any redemption path is a process run by people and systems. It can be slower than expected, gated, or suspended under stress — which is precisely when you want it. Because no redemption mechanic is stated at all, this path is the least specified of the three.

Who bears it: the holder. Neither Arch Network nor Arch Prime guarantees the reserve, and this page will not imply that either does.

## Reserve backing and market liquidity are different things

These two get conflated constantly, and the place the confusion becomes expensive is closeout.

**Reserve backing answers: if a path back through the issuer exists — and which entity that issuer is has not been stated — is there full value standing behind the unit at the end of it?** It is a solvency question. It is answered by the reserve and the custodian — and by a redemption process that has not been described.

**Market liquidity answers: what can I actually execute right now, in size, against a live order book?** It is an execution question. It is answered by who is bidding, at what depth, at this moment.

A fully backed dollar can trade below a dollar. Nothing about 1:1 backing puts a bid in the book. Backing tells you where the price should converge if a redemption process runs; liquidity tells you what you get if you cannot wait for it.

**Why this matters at closeout.** A liquidation does not go through a redemption queue. It sells into whatever market exists at that moment. So the number that determines the outcome of a closeout is the executable price, not the reserve ratio. This is also why the haircut framework treats price volatility, enforcement certainty, and the unwind route as three separate inputs rather than one — an asset can score well on backing and badly on the route out.

And the scope boundary on enforcement, which holds everywhere in this set: rule-based enforcement determines that closeout begins, on time and without needing anyone's cooperation. It does not determine the price at which closeout completes. That price is a market outcome.

The practical reading: size your archUSD exposure on the liquidity you can actually access under stress, not on the reserve ratio you can read on a dashboard.

## What does not hold

- **1:1 backing is not a price floor.** It describes what stands behind the unit. It does not put a bid in the market, and it does not survive a reserve asset that is itself impaired.
- **Permissionless mint is not permissionless redemption.** Mint is stated. Redemption is not stated at all — neither that it happens at par nor on what terms — and is flagged above.
- **archUSD is not a yield instrument, and this page states no yield figure for it.** There is no yield leg.
- **This page states no attestation cadence, no reserve breakdown, and no redemption SLA.** Those are undecided or unconfirmed, and they are flagged rather than estimated.
- **archUSD is not established as collateral in V1.** V1 collateral is native Bitcoin. Whether archUSD is registered as collateral, and at what haircut, is not stated.
