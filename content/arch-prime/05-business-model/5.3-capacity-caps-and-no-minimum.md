---
title: Capacity, caps and why there is no minimum
---


This page explains why Arch Prime has no minimum account size, what is actually scarce
instead, and the arithmetic that decides how much of it any one account may consume.

## There is no minimum

There is no minimum account size and no minimum ticket.

This is not a concession or a launch promotion. A minimum would contradict the reason Prime
exists. A prime broker that funds client financing off its own balance sheet has to charge
for the balance-sheet capacity each client consumes, which is why it sets a floor and why
self-custody accounts below that floor go unserved. Arch Prime is not underwriting each
client off its own balance sheet — client financing is supplied by third-party liquidity
providers — so the marginal client costs nothing to onboard, and there is no account size at
which serving a client stops making sense.

What is scarce is not Arch Prime's willingness to open an account. It is committed lending
depth.

## What is scarce, and how it is measured

Committed lending dollars, not code, gate the launch. Leverage capacity is a direct function
of stablecoin depth, so capacity is rationed rather than sold.

The arithmetic is small enough to do in your head, and it is worth doing, because it
explains every cap on this page.

A user seeking leverage λ consumes (λ − 1) dollars of borrow for every dollar of collateral
they post. At 2× leverage, a dollar of collateral needs a dollar of borrow. At 5× leverage,
the same dollar of collateral needs four. Leverage is not a setting on an account; it is a
claim on the shared pot of lent dollars, and the claim grows faster than the account does.

That gives the relationship the whole system is sized against:

```
supportable user collateral = (committed depth × utilisation) ÷ (leverage − 1)
```

Utilisation is the share of committed depth that is actually lent out. Target utilisation is
85%. The rate curve inflects at 90%, and 85% is chosen deliberately below that to preserve
withdrawal headroom for the lenders supplying the depth.

Two readings follow. Committed depth buys a lot of collateral if the cohort runs at low
leverage, and very little if it runs at high leverage. And the platform's capacity is set by
the leverage its users choose, not by the number of accounts it opens.

## The leverage table

Implied LTV rises with leverage, and an 80% max LTV puts a hard ceiling at 5.0×.

> **[OPEN — max LTV of 80% is the 21 August gameplan figure and is contested across the
> corpus (the canonical documentation, a separate internal analysis, and the parameters
> approved for the deployed market each carry a different value); it is stated here only
> with this flag until Risk reconciles · owner: Risk]**

| Leverage λ | Implied LTV | Borrow consumed per $1 of collateral (λ − 1) | Collateral supported per $1 of committed depth at 85% utilisation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.5× | ≈33% | $0.50 | $1.70 |
| 2.0× | 50% | $1.00 | $0.85 |
| 3.0× | ≈67% | $2.00 | $0.43 |
| 4.0× | 75% | $3.00 | $0.28 |
| 5.0× | 80% (ceiling) | $4.00 | $0.21 |

The right-hand column is the formula above, divided through by committed depth. It says that
moving the cohort from 2× to 5× cuts the collateral the same depth can serve by about three
quarters.

Worked once with a round number: take $100 million of committed depth purely to make the
arithmetic legible. At 85% utilisation, $85 million is available to lend. A cohort at 2.0×
leverage supports about $85 million of user collateral. The same depth at 4.0× supports
about $28 million.

**These are illustrative arithmetic, not commitments.** The dollar figures are a worked
example of the formula. They are not a depth target, a forecast, a capacity guarantee, or a
statement about what has been raised.

Leverage also relocates risk rather than adding capacity for free: risk moves to the
deployed leg, which is levered, which is why those positions are whitelisted, underwritten,
and liquidated first.

## How capacity is rationed: the per-account cap

Rationing is by per-account borrow cap, expressed as a share of available depth rather than
as a dollar amount. The recommendation is to start at 2% and step down as depth grows.

Expressing the cap as a share does one useful thing that a dollar cap cannot: it fixes the
cohort size arithmetically, at any depth. A cap of 2% is a ceiling on any single account —
no account can hold more than one fiftieth of the lent book — so the book admits 50 accounts
at full cap. Depth can double or halve and that count does not change. A dollar cap would
have to be renegotiated every time depth changed.

The cap also scales the individual account automatically. At $100 million of available depth
a 2% cap is $2 million of borrow — which, at 2.0× leverage, supports $2 million of
collateral, and at 5.0× supports about $500,000. Again: illustrative arithmetic on a round
number, not a commitment.

The step-down matters as much as the starting value. As depth grows, holding the cap at 2%
would let single accounts grow in absolute terms without limit, so the share is reduced as
the book gets larger. The number of accounts admitted at full cap rises with it.

> **[OPEN — the per-account borrow cap of 2% of available depth is a recommended starting
> value, not a ratified parameter, and the step-down schedule as depth grows is not set ·
> owner: Risk]**

## Why the cap is published rather than discretionary

The cap is a parameter, not a relationship. It is stated as a share, it is the same share
for every account, and it is checked in the borrow path — the same validation that checks
collateral and the requested borrow before any debt is issued — so it applies whether or not
anyone is watching.

That is what makes it useful to the people on both sides of it. A client can compute their
own ceiling before applying. A lender can compute worst-case single-account exposure to the
book they funded without asking Arch Prime for a client list. Neither calculation depends on
a discretionary decision that could be made differently for a large account on a busy day.

## What does not hold

- **No minimum is not open access.** Onboarding is gated by the capacity ladder. Early rungs
  are closed, and the cap is a share of a small book. See
  [Launch phases](5.4-launch-phases).
- **The cap rations borrow, not risk.** V1 runs one whitelisted strategy, which means 100%
  concentration by construction. Fifty accounts at full cap are fifty accounts in the same
  position. Per-strategy and per-manager caps arrive in phase two.
- **Utilisation is a target, not a control.** 85% is where the system is aimed. It is not a
  promise that headroom exists when a lender or a client wants out.
- **The leverage ceiling inherits a contested figure.** 5.0× is derived from the 80% max LTV
  above and moves if that number moves.
- **The table is arithmetic, not capacity.** Nothing in it states how much depth is
  committed today.
