---
title: Leverage and market risk
---


This page describes what leverage does to a Bitcoin position on Arch Prime, how a drawdown
reaches the health factor, and why the speed of a drawdown is a different problem from its
size.

## Where the leverage comes from

V1 gives you an experience that feels like a cross-margin account without being one
underneath. It uses isolated pools; you borrow against collateral, and leverage comes from
multi-instruction looping of those assets on a per-strategy basis. It is levered exposure
through isolated pools, not cross-margining. True portfolio margin with netted risk is V2.

Each loop is one atomic bundle: the system validates the collateral and the requested
borrow, issues the debt, deposits the proceeds into the whitelisted strategy, mints the
receipt directly into the margin account, registers it as a position, recomputes health
and asserts it — or the whole bundle reverts. You never hold the borrowed funds, and you
never hold the receipt in your own wallet. The enforcement property is possession, not
transferability: the receipt is a transferable instrument, and there is no step at which
you have possession of it. That is enforcement by transaction shape, not by policy, and it
means the leverage you take is always attached to a position the account can see and
reach.

## Implied LTV by leverage level

Leverage and loan-to-value are the same statement in two units. For target leverage λ, the
implied LTV of the looped position is `(λ − 1) / λ`:

| Target leverage | Implied LTV |
|---|---|
| 1.5× | ≈ 33% |
| 2.0× | 50% |
| 3.0× | ≈ 67% |
| 4.0× | 75% |
| 5.0× | 80% |

Max LTV in V1 is 80%. That puts a hard ceiling at 5.0×. The ceiling is not a policy
preference layered on top of the LTV — it is the same number read from the other end. You
cannot request 6× and be declined for style reasons; the bundle's health assertion fails
and the transaction reverts.

## How a drawdown reaches the health factor

The account computes:

```
collateralValue = Σ (quantity × price × (1 − haircut))
healthFactor    = collateralValue / totalDebt
```

Liquidation is triggered when the health factor falls below 1.

Debt does not move when the market does. Quantity does not move. Price does. So the health
factor is linear in the price of the collateral: a proportional fall of `x` in price
multiplies the health factor by `(1 − x)`.

Turned around: from a starting health factor `H`, the proportional decline in collateral
value that brings the account to the liquidation trigger is `(H − 1) / H`. Higher leverage
means a lower starting `H`, which means less room. That is the entire relationship, and it
is worth internalising because it is the one part of the account that behaves the way
intuition expects.

The haircut is what separates marked value from price. It is a function of price
volatility, enforcement certainty, and the unwind route available for the asset.
The haircut does not move with the market either — it is set in advance, which means it is
a bound on how the position is *valued*, not a bound on what the position *realizes*.

## Why a levered Bitcoin position is not the exposure you think it is

If you loop, the borrowed proceeds are deployed, and the deployed position sits in the
same account and the same health factor. Both legs are now live. A Bitcoin drawdown moves
one of them. A strategy impairment moves the other, and the other is larger.

This is the point at which most readers stop tracking their real exposure. The full
treatment is in [4.3 Strategy and NAV risk](./4.3-strategy-and-nav-risk), and it is the
page to read if you read only one.

Note also what additive margin means here: there is no correlation modelling in V1. If
your collateral and your deployed position both fall, you receive no offset for the fact
that they fell for the same reason, and no credit for any hedging relationship between
them. The margin engine adds; it does not net.

## Why a fast drawdown is different from a large one

A large drawdown is a number. A fast drawdown is a number arriving inside a window in
which several other things are also failing, and they fail together because they have the
same cause.

- **Valuation lag.** The account acts on the price it was last given. A move that happens
  between updates cannot be liquidated against, because as far as the health computation
  is concerned it has not happened. The gap between marked and true price is widest
  exactly when it matters. See [4.6](./4.6-price-and-oracle-risk).
- **The closeout is a process, not an instant.** The deployed leg is rank 1 and must be
  unwound to repay debt — sold into whatever book exists, or redeemed with the strategy.
  Either route takes as long as it takes, and the price keeps moving through it. See
  [4.4](./4.4-liquidity-and-redemption-risk).
- **Depth thins as you arrive.** Quoted depth in calm conditions is largely conditional —
  it is withdrawn in precisely the state that triggered your liquidation. A position that
  looked cheap to close when it was sized looks very different when it is closed.
- **Settlement congests.** A fast drawdown liquidates many accounts at once, which means
  many transactions competing for the same block space. Fee markets rise and settlement
  latency rises with them. See [4.8](./4.8-settlement-and-reorganization-risk).
- **You are not the only forced seller.** Every account at similar leverage crosses its
  trigger at roughly the same price. The liquidations arrive as a cohort, into the depth
  described above.

A large, slow decline gives all of those room to operate. A smaller, faster decline can
cost more than the larger slow one, because the mechanisms that were supposed to contain it are the
mechanisms the speed disables.

## Who bears it

The client bears market loss on the collateral. Rank ordering means the deployed leg is
closed before the Bitcoin is touched, and the Bitcoin is touched last — that changes the
order in which the loss is realized, not the fact of it.

If the closeout completes below the debt it was meant to repay, the shortfall is a credit
loss to the third-party liquidity providers who financed the position. Arch Prime does not
fund client financing.

## What reduces it

- The haircut, which values the position below its price, so the buffer is consumed before
  the headline move is fully reflected.
- The max-LTV ceiling, which caps the leverage available and therefore caps how thin the
  buffer can be made.
- The atomic health assertion in the bundle, which prevents an account being opened or
  extended into a state that already fails.
- Liquidation rank, which closes the levered deployed leg before reaching the Bitcoin.
- Per-account borrow caps expressed as a share of available depth, which limit how much of
  the market's liquidity a single account's liquidation must consume.

## What does not reduce it

- **Self-custody.** The client retains custody and enforcement does not require possession.
  Neither fact changes what Bitcoin's price does.
- **Enforcement certainty.** Rule-based enforcement determines that closeout begins, not
  the price at which it completes. It removes discretion from the timing, which is also
  what removes your option to wait out a move.
- **Additive margin.** No netting, no correlation offset, no diversification credit.
- **Rank ordering.** Bitcoin being rank 99 protects the sequence, not the balance.
- There is no stop-loss, no automatic de-levering below the trigger, and no bound on loss
  other than the size of the account.

## The residual

At any leverage above 1.0×, a Bitcoin drawdown can take your entire posted position, and a
fast one can take it at a price no parameter in this documentation described. The health
factor tells you where the trigger is. It does not tell you where the closeout clears, and
the distance between those two is unbounded in a market that is moving quickly.

Both of the numbers that would let you size that distance yourself — the liquidation
threshold and the reconciled max LTV — are flagged above as unresolved.
