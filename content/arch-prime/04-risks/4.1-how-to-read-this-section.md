This page states what the risk pages are, what they are not, and the one idea the rest of
the section is built on.

## What each page does

Every page in this section does five things in order: it names a failure mode, says who
absorbs it, says what reduces it, says what does not reduce it, and states what is left
over once everything that reduces it has been applied.

The fourth and fifth items are the reason the section exists. A page that lists a risk and
then lists a control has described the control. The residual — what remains after the
control works as designed — is the thing you are actually deciding about.

## What these pages are not

- **Not a complete list.** They cover the failure modes Arch Prime has identified, in the
  business it runs and in the network it runs on. A failure mode nobody has thought of is
  not disclosed here or anywhere.
- **Not architecture documentation.** The technical paper is authoritative on the
  protocol. These pages summarize it only where a risk turns on a mechanism, and they
  point upward when detail matters.
- **Not a statement of final parameters.** Where a number is not settled, the page says so
  and stops rather than estimating.
- **Not legal or investment advice.**

## The idea the section is built on

In a levered account on Arch Prime, the binding constraint is not Bitcoin's price. It is
the deployed position's NAV.

This is the single most important thing on these pages, and it is counter-intuitive for a
reader who came here because they hold Bitcoin. The account is opened with Bitcoin, the
Bitcoin is what the client cares about, and the Bitcoin is the last thing the system
touches — deployed strategy positions are liquidation rank 1, Bitcoin is rank 99. Every
part of that is true, and none of it makes Bitcoin's price the thing that decides the
outcome.

The arithmetic is short. For an account with equity `E` and a deployed position of value
`V`, the impairment in that position that wipes the equity is:

```
E / V
```

Leverage is what makes `V` larger than `E`. At 2.0× the deployed position is twice the
equity, and a 50% impairment ends the account. At 5.0× it is five times the equity, and a
20% impairment ends the account. The proportional move that destroys you shrinks in exact
proportion to the leverage you chose.

Bitcoin's price moves against a number the size of your equity. The strategy's NAV moves
against a number several times larger. A small percentage of a large number beats a large
percentage of a small one, and that is the whole of it.

This is also why the capital efficiency of the account is not free. The efficiency comes
almost entirely from counting the deployed position rather than from netting — V1 margin
is deliberately additive and there is no correlation modelling. Risk moves to the deployed
leg, which is levered, which is why those positions are whitelisted, underwritten, and
liquidated first.

[4.3 Strategy and NAV risk](./4.3-strategy-and-nav-risk) works this through with an
illustration. Everything else in the section is downstream of it: leverage sets the ratio
([4.2](./4.2-leverage-and-market-risk)), unwindability — by sale or by redemption —
decides whether the deployed leg can be reached in time
([4.4](./4.4-liquidity-and-redemption-risk)), the manager
decides what the NAV does ([4.5](./4.5-counterparty-risk)), and the oracle decides what
the account believes the NAV is ([4.6](./4.6-price-and-oracle-risk)).

## The boundary that runs through everything

Deterministic enforcement is a claim about the certainty and timing of the recovery
*process*, never about the *price* at which recovery occurs.

Read that sentence as the qualifier attached to every enforcement claim in this
documentation, including the ones on these pages. What the rules determine is that a
closeout begins, that it begins when the health factor says it should, and that it does not
depend on anyone choosing to cooperate. What the rules do not determine is what the position is
worth when the closeout completes.

The two are unrelated properties, and conflating them is the most expensive mistake
available to a reader of this documentation. A liquidation that fires exactly on schedule
into a market that has fallen a long way since the trigger has performed correctly and cost you
the money anyway.

Nothing in this section walks that back later.

## How to read the flags

Where a number or a decision does not exist yet, the page carries an inline flag: a bolded
block, opening with the word `OPEN`, stating in one line what is missing and naming the
role that owns closing it. The flags are indexed in the open-items register, and this page
deliberately contains none of its own so that the register stays accurate.

A flag means the item is unresolved, not that it is being withheld. These pages are
allowed to ship with flags. They are not allowed to ship with a figure invented to fill
one. Where you see a flag on a parameter that matters to your decision, the correct
inference is that the decision cannot be fully made yet.

## Who bears what

- **The client** bears market risk on the collateral, NAV risk on the deployed position,
  and execution risk in liquidation. In the ordinary case, the client's loss is bounded by
  the client's equity, and the rank ordering determines the sequence in which positions
  are closed — not whether the account can go to zero.
- **The lenders who finance the book** bear the shortfall if a closeout completes below
  the debt it was meant to repay. Arch Prime does not fund client financing; third-party
  liquidity providers do. A credit loss lands on them.
- **Arch Prime** holds whitelisting authority over the strategy and parameter authority over
  haircuts, caps and ranks, and it underwrites the counterparties it onboards. Those are
  powers, and the risks that follow from them are Arch Prime's to disclose and the client's
  to carry.
- **Arch Network** holds the collateral, authorizes any movement of it, and settles to
  Bitcoin. Upgrade authority over its programs sits above the account rules, and who holds it
  is unspecified. Those risks have a different owner and different failure modes from the
  ones above, and neither party's conduct bounds the other's. See
  [4.7](./4.7-protocol-validator-and-upgrade-risk).

## What reduces it

The controls that run across the whole section, each of which the individual pages take up
in detail:

- **The haircut**, which values a position below its price before it counts as collateral,
  and which reads price volatility, enforcement certainty and the unwind route.
- **The max-LTV ceiling**, which bounds the leverage available and therefore how thin the
  buffer can be made.
- **The atomic health assertion in the bundle**, which reverts any borrow-and-deploy that
  would leave the account in a state that already fails.
- **Liquidation rank**, which unwinds the levered deployed leg first and reaches the
  client's Bitcoin last.
- **Whitelisting and hand-underwriting of the deployed strategy**, which is what makes 100%
  concentration in V1 survivable rather than acceptable.
- **The per-account borrow cap**, expressed as a share of available depth, which limits how
  much of the market's liquidity any single account's closeout must consume.

None of these bounds the price at which a closeout completes. They bound what can be built,
and the order in which it is taken apart.

## What does not reduce any of it

Self-custody. The client retains custody and enforcement does not require possession, and
that property is real — but it is a property of the Bitcoin leg. It does nothing about the
NAV of the deployed position, the solvency of a stablecoin issuer, the accuracy of a price
input, or the price at which a liquidation clears. Most of what can cost you money in a
levered account is not a custody question.

## The residual

After every control described in this section works exactly as designed, a client of Arch
Prime can lose the entire Bitcoin position they posted, through a strategy impairment that
was smaller than the price moves they were comfortable holding Bitcoin through.

That outcome is not a malfunction. It is the arithmetic of `E / V` operating normally, and
it is available at every leverage level above 1.0×.
