title: Buying power and borrowing

This page explains where your buying power comes from, what caps it, and why the cap is
sometimes about your account and sometimes about the size of the book.

## Where buying power comes from

Buying power is the borrowing your account's haircut collateral will support. Arch Prime
derives it rather than granting it:

```
collateralValue = Σ (quantity × price × (1 − haircut))
buying power    = what can be borrowed against collateralValue, subject to
                  max LTV, the per-account borrow cap, and available depth
```

Three limits apply in order, and the binding one is whichever is smallest.

## Limit one: max LTV

Arch Prime sets max LTV at 80% in V1. That is the ceiling on borrowing against your posted
collateral, before any other constraint applies.

## Limit two: leverage, and what it implies about LTV

Leverage in V1 comes from multi-instruction looping of the deployed asset. Each level of
leverage implies a loan-to-value on the resulting position:

| Target leverage | Implied LTV |
|---|---|
| 1.5× | ≈ 33% |
| 2.0× | 50% |
| 3.0× | ≈ 67% |
| 4.0× | 75% |

An 80% max LTV puts a hard ceiling at 5.0×. There is no configuration in V1 that exceeds
it, and the ceiling is arithmetic, not policy.

Read the table in the direction that costs you money: 4× is not "a bit more than 3×." It is
a position where a 25% impairment in the deployed leg exhausts the equity supporting it.

## Limit three: the per-account borrow cap and the size of the book

This is the limit most readers do not expect, and it is the one that binds at launch.

Arch Prime does not fund client financing. Third-party liquidity providers do, and Arch
Prime earns a spread on flow it does not finance. So the amount of borrowing available is a
direct function of how many committed lending dollars exist. Committed lending dollars, not
code, gate the launch: the rollout is a capacity ladder, not a date.

Arch Prime rations across accounts with a per-account borrow cap expressed as a share of
available depth. The recommendation is to start at 2% and step down as depth grows. A 2%
cap admits 50 accounts at full cap — which is the point of it: the cap exists so that
capacity reaches a cohort of accounts rather than two of them.

### The capacity arithmetic

How much collateral the book can support depends on how much leverage its users want,
because leverage is what consumes borrowing:

```
supportable user collateral = (committed depth × utilisation) ÷ (leverage − 1)
```

A user seeking leverage λ consumes (λ − 1) dollars of borrow per dollar of collateral. At
2× that is one dollar borrowed per dollar posted; at 4× it is three. The same book serves
three times fewer dollars of collateral when its users move from 2× to 4×.

Target utilisation is 85%. The rate curve inflects at 90%, and running at 85% preserves
withdrawal headroom — the book is deliberately not run to the top, so that lenders can
exit and accounts can unwind without the rate spiking against everyone.

## There is no minimum

There is no minimum account size and no minimum ticket. A minimum would contradict the
reason Arch Prime exists: because Arch Prime is not underwriting each client off its own
balance sheet, the marginal client costs nothing to onboard, which is exactly what lets a
small self-custody account be served when a balance-sheet prime cannot serve one.

Arch Prime's per-account cap limits how much of the book any one account can take. It does
not set a floor under how small an account can be.

## Limits

- **Buying power is not a credit line.** It is recomputed from marks and haircuts
  continuously; it can fall without you doing anything.
- **The capacity ladder can move against you.** Available depth is a function of committed
  lending, and both the per-account cap and the depth behind it are subject to change as
  rungs open.
- **Borrowing runs through isolated pools.** Leverage on one strategy does not draw on, or
  offset against, a position in another. Netting is V2.
- **There is no path that hands you the borrowed funds.** Buying power is deployable, not
  withdrawable. See
  [Deploying: the yield marketplace](2.4-deploying-the-yield-marketplace).
- **Capital efficiency here has a cost.** Risk moves to the deployed leg, which is levered,
  which is why those positions are whitelisted, underwritten, and liquidated first.
